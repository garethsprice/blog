In 2023 a student at the University of North Georgia was accused of cheating after an AI-detection tool flagged her essay. She said she had written it herself. The trigger turned out to be Grammarly. The university later cleared her[^1]. The episode matters not because it was rare, but because it exposed a problem institutions are already punishing people for: we have no stable way to describe where editing ends and authorship begins.

Machine assistance in writing now ranges from spell-checking to near-total automation. Yet almost all of it is discussed under a single label: “AI-generated content.” The term collapses distinctions that matter. A dyslexic founder refining an investor memo with ChatGPT and a content farm publishing fabricated medical advice are, under current usage, doing the same thing. The category is broad enough to be technically accurate and useless at the same time.

When rules depend on a category this vague, enforcement follows a familiar pattern. The worst actors evade scrutiny by operating at scale, while visible individuals absorb the penalties. That is not a failure of detection. It is a failure of definition.

There is a simpler line to draw. Focus on who remains responsible for the claims being made.

Machine-assisted writing sits on a spectrum. At one end is ordinary authorship using familiar tools. At the other is full automation: systems that generate substantive claims with minimal review, often at scale, sometimes with no expectation that a human will even read the output. Between those poles lies a growing practice that is neither editing nor automation and deserves to be named.

Call it _syngraphy_ (_from the Greek_ **syn**, together, and **graphein**, to write): collaborative composition in which a human author retains responsibility for propositions and judgment, while a model assists with language, structure, or exploration. The system may write sentences. The claims that survive remain the author’s responsibility.

That definition matters because it clarifies what syngraphy is not. It is not prompt-to-publish generation where the user cannot explain the output. It is not fabrication disguised as efficiency. The distinction does not rest on how much text a model produces, but on who owns the judgment behind it.

The cost of getting this wrong runs in both directions. By late 2024 NewsGuard had identified more than a thousand websites publishing AI-written material designed to mimic legitimate journalism; that number has since doubled[^2].
 At the same time, peer-reviewed research shows that AI-detection tools routinely misclassify human writing, disproportionately flagging non-native English speakers[^3].
 The result is predictable: large-scale abuse persists, while enforcement falls on the people least able to contest it.

The access question cuts deeper than most commentary admits. Long-form writing is still the gate through which serious intellectual work must pass. Tools that lower the mechanical cost of clear prose can widen that gate, allowing people with strong ideas and imperfect English to participate on closer to equal terms. That benefit holds only if responsibility for claims stays with the author. When judgment is displaced rather than supported, assistance becomes corrosive.

The calculator analogy is often invoked here, and it is partly useful. Calculators reduced the cost of arithmetic without absolving students of understanding. Language models can do something similar for writing. But unlike calculators, they produce probabilistic output: plausible sentences that may contain subtle errors, invented citations, or confident nonsense. A model can function like a calculator for language only if the human retains the higher-order role—deciding what is true, what follows, and what the evidence supports.

Some uses should plainly be restricted: publishing claims no one has verified, impersonating expertise, submitting generated work where the point is demonstrating a skill. But restrictions are enforceable only when we can say clearly what they are being distinguished from. Vagueness benefits no one except the actors who were never going to follow the rules.

That student in Georgia deserved a word for what she actually did, and a different one for what she was accused of doing. So do the millions of people using these tools not to avoid thinking, but to get their thinking onto the page. The gap in our vocabulary is not a semantic quibble. It shapes what institutions can govern, and who gets punished while they work it out.
