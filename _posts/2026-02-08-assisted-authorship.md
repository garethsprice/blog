A colleague read a strategy document I had written and sent me a message: "Did you write this?"

It is a yes-or-no question. My answer took a paragraph.

Yes, with caveats. The process was: deep research on where our market is heading, scenario planning, adversarial stress-testing of the arguments with AI, then an aggressive editorial pass with a style guide designed for tight, clean writing. Could I have done it without AI? Maybe, given a month or two. But the document was not just faster to produce. It was better — sharper, tighter, cleaner — because it had been through an editorial process that I, as a solo writer, could never have afforded.

This is the opposite of what researchers have started calling "workslop" — AI-generated content that "masquerades as good work, but lacks the substance to meaningfully advance a given task."[^1] The researchers distinguish between "pilots," who steer AI with judgment, and "passengers," who let AI carry them to avoid the hard parts of thinking. Piloting often takes just as long as writing from scratch. The gain is not in hours. It is in quality.

"Lazy thinking is still lazy thinking, just amplified," I wrote to my colleague. "But is good thinking also amplified?" Their response: "Lazy thinking isn't thinking. It's compulsion. Tools are neutral and their capabilities are completely subjective to the wielder." If what matters is the rigor of the thinking rather than the mechanics of the typing, then authorship does not mean what I thought it meant.

### The fiction of working alone

I assumed authorship meant: one person, working alone, producing text that reflects their thinking in their words. Then I looked at how authorship actually works.

J.R. Moehringer wrote Andre Agassi's *Open*, Phil Knight's *Shoe Dog*, and Prince Harry's *Spare*. Agassi had offered Moehringer a co-author credit; Moehringer declined[^2]. Then, watching Agassi accept praise on a late-night talk show, Moehringer yelled at the television: "Say my name! Say my f---ing name!"[^3] Moehringer wrote every word. Agassi is the author.

About a third of Shakespeare's plays contain writing by someone other than Shakespeare[^4]. Political speeches are written by staffers. Op-eds are drafted by policy aides. The social contract is not that the named author did the writing. It is that the named author takes responsibility for the claims. It was the standard I held myself to — a standard that almost nobody meets and almost nobody enforces, until a machine made the collaboration visible.

### Authorship is responsibility

Agassi is the author of *Open* because Agassi's name is on the cover and Agassi bears the consequences of what it contains. If the book included a fabrication, Agassi — not Moehringer — would answer for it.

In 2023, a New York lawyer submitted a brief drafted with ChatGPT that cited six cases the model had invented; the court imposed sanctions[^5]. The lawyer had not checked the claims. They used the tool to replace their thinking entirely. The same year, a student at the University of North Georgia was accused of cheating after an AI-detection tool flagged their essay. They had used Grammarly to clean up the text. The university later cleared them[^6]. But we have no vocabulary that distinguishes what the student did from what the lawyer did. "AI-generated content" covers both.

These are not the same thing. The difference is not the tool. The difference is whether the human can explain, defend, and take responsibility for the result.

Can I defend every claim in this essay? Yes. Did I write every sentence? No. A model helped me draft, restructure, and refine. But every claim is one I believe, every piece of evidence is one I verified, and every judgment is one I made. If something here is wrong, it is my fault. The model has no stakes. I do.

### Amplification, not delegation

This emerging practice — iterative, conversational, moving between reading and writing and revising at a level that was not possible alone — needs a name. Call it **assisted authorship**: composition in which a human retains responsibility for propositions and judgment, while a model assists with language, structure, or exploration. The system may write the sentences. The claims that survive remain the author's responsibility. Steve Jobs called the computer a bicycle for the mind. AI is a motorcycle for the mind: more dangerous, easier to crash, but you can go so much further.

I used AI to write this essay. I am the author. Next time someone asks "did you write this?" I want to be able to say yes without caveats. Not because the process has changed — but because the word finally caught up with the work.
