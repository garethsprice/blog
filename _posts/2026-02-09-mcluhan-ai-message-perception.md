Every technology humans have built to extend the mind has had the decency to sit still while we studied it. The book reorganised human consciousness over centuries, replacing oral memory with fixed text, communal knowledge with private reading, and it did all of this while remaining the same object on every desk. Television rewired political culture and elevated image over argument, but it did not change its broadcast based on your facial expression. The printing press, the telegraph, the radio, the telephone: each restructured how humans perceive, think, and organise themselves, and each held still long enough for us to understand what it was doing.

AI does not hold still. It watches you use it, models your patterns, and adjusts its responses so seamlessly that the restructuring feels like service. This is the most important thing about the technology, and it has no precedent.

Marshall McLuhan, the Canadian media theorist who coined "the medium is the message" and "the global village," argued that the message of any medium is not its content but its structural effect on human perception.[^3] Print's message was the linear, individualist consciousness it fostered. Television's was the replacement of argument with image. McLuhan never saw AI, but his framework identifies what makes it different from every medium before it: its structural effects are *personalised*. The restructuring is tailored to each user, invisible to each user, and different for each user. That is its message. Your reality is yours alone, your cognition is a private service, your version of knowledge need not be anyone else's. Individuation at scale, as legible through McLuhan's lens as print's message of rationality or television's message of the image.

Consider what happens when you use AI to think through a problem. The interface is conversational. The experience feels like collaboration. But the collaborator is a statistical engine that has modelled the most probable completion of your input, calibrated to feel satisfying to you. The medium is optimised to be invisible as a medium. Every previous technology McLuhan analysed had formal properties observable from the outside: the book as object, the television as format. AI's formal properties shift with each interaction. The water changes temperature for every fish.

This creates a cognitive effect worth naming: the participation illusion. McLuhan identified something similar with television, which he called a "cool" medium because it demanded viewers fill in the low-resolution image. But television's invitation to participate was structural and uniform. Everyone filled in the same gaps. AI's invitation is adaptive and personal. You feel like a co-author. Research bears this out: in studies of human-AI text production, users who had limited influence over AI-generated output did not feel ownership of it, yet publicly declared themselves as authors; those given more control reported stronger ownership regardless of how much the model contributed.[^1] The gap between felt authorship and actual authorship is not a bug. It is the medium's message. This is not a question of capacity. A critical thinker can engage AI as an active interlocutor and produce something genuinely new. The question is whether the medium's adaptive responsiveness lets people *believe* they are doing that work when they are not.

McLuhan held that every extension is also an amputation.[^4] The wheel extended the foot and amputated the need to walk. The book extended memory and amputated the oral tradition. With AI, the amputation is harder to see, because it is happening to the very faculty you would use to notice it. When reasoning becomes a service you consume rather than a process you perform, the capacity for independent reasoning does not announce its departure. It just quietly stops being exercised. This is no longer speculative. A 2025 MIT Media Lab study measured brain activity in participants writing essays over four months, comparing LLM users, search engine users, and those writing unaided.[^2] LLM users showed the weakest neural connectivity of the three groups, with cognitive engagement scaling down in proportion to tool reliance. When reassigned to write without assistance, their brains showed reduced alpha and beta connectivity: the neural signature of under-engagement. The atrophy was directional. It did not reverse on demand. LLM users also reported the lowest sense of ownership over their essays, and could not accurately quote their own work.

I build AI products, and these findings change how I think about what I am building. The default instinct in the industry is to minimise friction: give users the answer, reduce the steps, make it seamless. McLuhan's framework suggests this instinct has a cost no product metric captures. Every design decision that reduces cognitive friction also reduces cognitive engagement. An AI copilot that presents a finished analysis and one that presents a hypothesis for interrogation produce the same deliverable. They do not produce the same user. The first makes the tool indispensable by making the person less capable without it. The second makes the person more capable whether the tool is present or not. Most of the industry is building the first, because it demos better and reduces support costs. The result is an optimisation for adoption that externalises the cognitive debt onto every user.

Anyone building AI products should be asking questions that do not appear on any product roadmap. Does your interface show its reasoning, or only its conclusions? When a user accepts an AI-generated output without modification, does the system notice? Are you measuring what your users are learning, or only what they are producing? If your product were withdrawn tomorrow, would your users be more capable than when they started, or less? These are design decisions with measurable consequences. The Kosmyna study shows what those consequences look like at the neural level after four months. The companies that treat cognitive engagement as a design constraint will build products that last. The rest will build dependencies.

AI is not more powerful than previous cognitive technologies. It is structurally *different*. Every prior tool, from the abacus to the search engine, extended a specific faculty while leaving the rest of the mind to operate independently. You could outsource arithmetic without outsourcing judgment. You could outsource memory retrieval without outsourcing reasoning. AI sits across all cognitive functions simultaneously, inside an interface that feels like thinking itself. The difference is between a tool that augments a limb and an environment that surrounds the whole body.

McLuhan's global village offers the final lens. He predicted that electronic media would retribalise humanity, not into harmony but into the intense, fractious intimacy of village life at planetary scale. He was describing Twitter in 1964. AI adds a personalised oracle at the centre that tells each petitioner something different, shaped by their history and biases. The village still shares a square, but everyone hears a different sermon. McLuhan imagined a shared media environment producing different interpretations. He did not imagine one producing *different realities tailored to each inhabitant*. The fragmentation is no longer interpretive. It is infrastructural. Anyone building AI products that inform decisions in medicine, finance, policy, or education has an obligation to consider how their system maintains contact with a shared, verifiable reality rather than constructing a comfortable private one.

None of this is invisible because people are failing to pay attention. It is invisible because invisibility is the medium's primary feature. Print announced itself: you picked up a book. Television announced itself: you turned on the set. AI integrates. It slips into your email client, your search engine, your word processor, your children's homework. It is not a new channel. It is a new layer on all existing channels, and that makes it the hardest medium in history to see from the outside.

The question is not whether AI is reshaping cognition. Every powerful medium does. The question is whether a medium whose message is *your reality is yours alone* is compatible with any form of shared public life. There is a separate inquiry into what AI means for how we create and interpret meaning, for authorship, for reading, for the encounter between a mind and a text. That is a literary question, and it deserves its own answer. But the perceptual question comes first, because it determines the conditions under which meaning-making happens at all. McLuhan built the best toolkit we have for understanding media's effects on the mind. It is not broken by AI. It works. And what it diagnoses is a medium designed to make every user feel uniquely served while quietly dissolving the common perceptual ground on which collective reasoning depends.
