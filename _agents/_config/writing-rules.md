# Writing Rules

**Opening:** State the main claim in the first paragraph. Or open with a short, concrete scenario that puts the reader's problem on the page, then state the thesis in the second paragraph. No atmosphere, no warm-up, no aphorism that compresses the thesis into a slogan.

**Closing:** Stop when the argument is complete. Do not restate. End by recontextualising, elevating or complicating, or with a concrete next step. No zinger engineered to be quoted; an image may close only if it is attached to something the reader now understands or can do.

**Headers:** Carry a claim or name the question the section answers ("Where delegation breaks"). Never a bare topic label. Eight words or fewer. No compound claims, no counts ("Eight routines…", "four ways").

**Paragraphs:** One idea. Lead with the claim; one in four can lead with evidence. Every paragraph load-bearing — if the reader would not miss it, cut it.

**Evidence:** Place at point of need. Replace adjectives with data. Cite naturally — "Snowflake's benchmarks show 85%" not "According to a recent study."

**Precedents:** One per point — the strongest. A second must add a different lesson, not the same one from another century. Follow each historical example or quotation with one sentence stating what it means for the reader's case. If you can't write that sentence, cut the precedent.

**Quotations:** Quote only when the exact wording matters. Paraphrase archaic, translated or bureaucratic wording. Name the speaker before the quote.

**Sentences:** Vary rhythm. Active voice. Cut connective filler. Prefer positive construction. No runs of three or more short declaratives — join them with a participle, colon or semicolon.

**Terms:** Don't coin labels ("action class", "autonomy ratchet"). If a term needs a bracketed definition, write the definition instead.

**Length:** Standard 600–1,500 words; practical guides 1,000–1,500; extended analysis up to 2,500. Research surplus is not a reason to run long.

**The generic-blog test:** If a sentence could appear on any tech leadership blog unchanged, rewrite until it couldn't.

**Mechanism before interpretation:** State what happened before what it means. The reader must always distinguish evidence from inference.

## Practical guides (content type: framework or practical guide)

- Thesis is the reader's decision: "Decide what an overnight agent may do while you're asleep."
- One worked example, introduced in the opening, applied in every section. When advising what to write (prompt, policy, config), show a short example of it.
- Address the reader as "you". No invented role nouns ("the operator", "the approver").
- Headers name the task or question: "Explain what success means."
- Precedents illustrate; they are not the spine. A section that is mostly history has drifted.
- End with an action the reader can take before the next run.
