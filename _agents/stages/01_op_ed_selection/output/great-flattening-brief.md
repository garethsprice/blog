# Brief: The Great Flattening

**Topic:** The Great Flattening — the collapse of cultural time into a single, simultaneous plane of access, the emergence of AI as both audience and producer, and what happens when culture accelerates past the human ability to make meaning from it.

**Angle:** Streaming, digital distribution, and AI haven't just made culture more accessible — they've destroyed the temporal hierarchy that gave culture its structure. Everything is available at once: new competes with old on equal footing, no IP ever dies, and the result is what Baudrillard would recognise as the hyperreal — a world of copies with no original, where the map precedes the territory. David Bowie predicted music would become "like running water" — commodity infrastructure, not event. He was right, and the implications extend far beyond music. McLuhan told us the medium is the message — so what is the message of AI? If every previous medium restructured perception (print made thought linear, TV made it mosaic), AI's message may be the flattening itself: the abolition of sequence, hierarchy, and provenance as organising principles.

**Thesis seed:** The flattening of culture into a single accessible plane was only the first phase — the next is AI producing culture for itself, accelerating it past human tempo, and throwing off strange artifacts into our world like exhaust from an engine that no longer needs passengers.

**Primary audience:** Informed generalists interested in technology, culture, and intellectual history (tertiary audience for the blog — this is a philosophy piece that earns its relevance through the practical implications).

**Content type:** Opinion / philosophical essay

**Word count target:** 2,200–3,000

**Key points to cover:**

1. **The Bowie prediction fulfilled** — "Music itself is going to become like running water or electricity" (2002). Not nostalgia — structural analysis. He saw that digital distribution would strip music of scarcity and event-ness. Apply this to all cultural production.

2. **McLuhan's question applied to AI** — "The medium is the message" means every medium reshapes perception before anyone notices the content. Print linearised thought. TV made it mosaic and simultaneous. The internet dissolved geography. So what does AI do? It dissolves *sequence*. An AI doesn't know what came first, what influenced what, what's canonical and what's derivative — and it doesn't care. That indifference *is* the message. The medium that processes all of culture treats all of culture as equally weighted input.

3. **Baudrillard's hyperreality arrives via the recommendation algorithm** — Simulacra and Simulation argued that representations would replace reality. The streaming catalogue is the simulacrum: no original context, no chronology, no distinction between the canonical and the derivative. The algorithm flattens further — it doesn't know (or care) that one thing came first. McLuhan and Baudrillard converge here: the medium's message (flattening) produces the hyperreal (a world where the copy has no original to refer back to).

4. **No IP ever dies** — Every film, song, book, and game remains in circulation permanently. The long tail isn't a niche phenomenon; it's the entire competitive landscape. New work doesn't replace old work; it competes with it. The archive is not a library — it's an arena.

5. **The collapse of temporal hierarchy** — Culture used to have a natural ordering: what's current, what's classic, what's forgotten. Digital access removed the friction that created those categories. A teenager's playlist is 2024 and 1977 and 2003 simultaneously, with no sense that these are different eras. Time flattens into a single browsable surface.

6. **The fourth-order simulacrum: AI as audience and producer** — Baudrillard described four stages of simulation: reflection of reality → masking of reality → masking the *absence* of reality → pure simulation with no relation to reality at all. We are entering the fourth. AI systems are already generating content trained on content trained on content — and increasingly they will produce culture not for human consumption but as input for other AI systems. When AI makes content for AI, the human audience becomes incidental. Culture accelerates beyond human tempo, and what leaks back into human culture arrives as strange artifacts — generated music that optimises for algorithmic placement, synthetic images that reference synthetic images, text that answers questions no human asked. The feedback loop no longer passes through human meaning-making at all. This is Baudrillard's fourth order made literal: simulation that has forgotten it was ever simulating something.

7. **Implications for makers** — If novelty can't win on novelty alone (because the audience has infinite alternatives), what does win? Possibly: presence, liveness, community, scarcity manufactured through experience rather than access. Or possibly: nothing wins, and culture becomes an undifferentiated flow — Bowie's running water made literal. The fourth-order question sharpens this: makers aren't just competing with the archive and with each other — they're competing with a machine culture that produces at inhuman speed and doesn't need an audience to justify its output.

**Differentiation statement:** The author has built content recommendation and discovery systems at The New York Times and Codecademy — understands the flattening from the supply side (how algorithms erase provenance and chronology) as well as the consumption side. The Baudrillard framing isn't decorative; it's the most precise theoretical framework for what's actually happening in production systems.
